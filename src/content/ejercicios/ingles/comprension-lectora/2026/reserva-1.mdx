<p class="en-heading">POTLATCH</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">The word <em>potlatch</em> comes from a Nootkan term meaning “to give.” Among the Indigenous peoples of the Northwest Coast of</li>
  <li>North America, it refers to a grand ceremonial feast marking major life events such as births, marriages, or deaths. The name</li>
  <li>reflects the central custom of these gatherings, in which hosts distribute their wealth to guests as a means of reinforcing social</li>
  <li>status and community ties.</li>
  <li class="is-start">The details of organizing and carrying out a potlatch varied from one regional group to another. Preparations could take months</li>
  <li>or even years. Potlatches were held in a ceremonial Big House, whose size reflected the host’s status within the village. Chiefs</li>
  <li>with the largest Big Houses often invited hundreds of guests from neighboring Indigenous communities. Guests traveled to the</li>
  <li>potlatch by canoe and, upon arrival, announced themselves and their home villages by calling out to the host on shore. Sometimes</li>
  <li>so many canoes arrived that there was hardly any space left along the beach. Each invited guest was fed, housed, and entertained,</li>
  <li>and often received many valuable gifts throughout the event, which could last from several days to several weeks, depending on</li>
  <li>the hosts’ status and the size of their community.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Beyond being a celebration, the potlatch played a vital role in community life by affirming social hierarchy and status through</li>
  <li>the generous distribution of wealth among guests. To maintain their honor after receiving gifts, guests were expected to host their</li>
  <li>own potlatch and present offerings of even greater value than those they had received.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Early European colonizers seeking to promote the fur trade with local tribes also took part in potlatches, distributing items such</li>
  <li>as blankets, jewelry, musical instruments, clothing, furniture, and sacks of flour. In time, however, both colonizers and Christian</li>
  <li>missionaries came to condemn the potlatch as wasteful and incompatible with Western values that associated wealth with success.</li>
  <li>Seeing it as an obstacle to the cultural assimilation and economic progress of Indigenous peoples, the Canadian government</li>
  <li>forbade the practice in 1884. From an Indigenous perspective, the ban prevented communities from performing essential social</li>
  <li>and spiritual obligations.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Despite the prohibition, potlatches continued in secret, often masked as other gatherings or held in remote villages. In 1886, a</li>
  <li>Tsimshian chief hosted a potlatch in British Columbia. The goods to be given away were hidden behind a partition in the house.</li>
  <li>The pile of goods was so high that the roof boards were removed to accommodate it. Similar acts of quiet disobedience occurred</li>
  <li>everywhere along the coast, as communities found creative ways to preserve their traditions under the ban.</li>
  <li class="is-start">After the ban was lifted in 1951, the ceremony regained visibility and strength. Today, the potlatch endures as a symbol of</li>
  <li>cultural identity, resistance, and continuity among the Indigenous peoples of the Northwest Coast.</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-question"><strong>A1. According to the text, potlatches…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) were held every year.</span>
  <span>(b) strengthened the bonds among tribal families.</span>
  <span>(c) disappeared after they were banned.</span>
  <span>(d) were held when a new chief was appointed.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(b) strengthened the bonds among tribal families.</strong></p>
  <p>Los anfitriones repartían su riqueza para reforzar los lazos de la comunidad (líneas 3-4): <em>“hosts distribute their wealth to guests as a means of reinforcing social status and community ties.”</em></p>
  <p>No se celebraban cada año, sino en grandes acontecimientos como nacimientos, bodas o muertes (a y d); y siguieron celebrándose en secreto tras la prohibición (<em>“potlatches continued in secret”</em>) (c).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-question"><strong>A2. According to the text, the celebrations…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) were prepared long in advance.</span>
  <span>(b) were reserved for a privileged few.</span>
  <span>(c) included goodbye rituals.</span>
  <span>(d) lasted longer when more guests attended.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(a) were prepared long in advance.</strong></p>
  <p>La preparación podía durar meses o incluso años (líneas 5-6): <em>“Preparations could take months or even years.”</em></p>
  <p>Se invitaba a cientos de personas de otras comunidades (<em>“hundreds of guests”</em>) (b); el texto no habla de rituales de despedida (c); y la duración dependía del estatus del anfitrión y del tamaño de su comunidad, no del número de invitados (d).</p>
</Solucion>
