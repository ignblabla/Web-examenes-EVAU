<p class="en-heading">THE THEFT OF THE CENTURY</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">Last October, as the sun rose over Paris and visitors began queueing through the Louvre Museum’s corridors, one of the most</li>
  <li>shocking thefts in recent memory took place. Within eight minutes, a gang of thieves sped away from the heart of the French</li>
  <li>capital with some of the nation’s most valuable treasures.</li>
  <li class="is-start">According to French authorities, the gang that broke into the Apollo Gallery, just a short walk from the Mona Lisa, was clearly</li>
  <li>professional. They knew exactly what they were after. Parking a lorry equipped with an elevating platform like those used by</li>
  <li>removal companies, they lifted themselves to a first-floor balcony and used a disc cutter to gain entry to the gallery.</li>
  <li class="is-start">The thieves did not target world-famous paintings that could never be displayed or sold. Instead, they went for items that could</li>
  <li>be converted into cash. They made straight for two display cases containing what remains of the French crown jewels, taking eight</li>
  <li>items worth an estimated €88 million, including diadems, necklaces, earrings, and brooches. During the escape, there was a</li>
  <li>moment of carelessness: they dropped Empress Eugénie’s exquisite 19th-century crown.</li>
  <li class="is-start">In spite of their historical and cultural value, the jewels—officially described as priceless—can be broken up and sold piece by</li>
  <li>piece. Even large, famous diamonds can be recut. The final price of the stolen jewels may be smaller, but it would still be</li>
  <li>considerable.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Despite its 230-year history, there have been relatively few thefts at the Louvre, which houses thousands of artworks famous</li>
  <li>around the world. This low number of thefts is largely due to the museum’s tight security. Reflecting on the incident, a gallery</li>
  <li>attendant on duty said, “For us, it was heartbreaking to see the display cases broken.” And she added, “Never for a moment did</li>
  <li>we think there was such a risk; nobody can be prepared for that.” She was right: the only real precedent was the notorious 1911</li>
  <li>disappearance of Leonardo da Vinci’s <em>La Joconde</em>, better known as the <em>Mona Lisa</em>. The thief hid in a closet overnight, removed</li>
  <li>the painting from its frame, wrapped it in his jacket, tucked it under his arm, and simply walked out. He turned out to be an Italian</li>
  <li>nationalist who believed da Vinci’s masterpiece belonged in Italy. The painting was recovered three years later and returned to the</li>
  <li>Louvre.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Since the October incident, security measures have been reinforced across France’s cultural institutions. The Louvre has</li>
  <li>transferred some of its most precious jewels to the Bank of France following the theft, which has been generally regarded as a</li>
  <li>source of embarrassment for the French government.</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-task">CHOOSE AND WRITE THE CORRECT OPTION (A, B, C or D). (0.5 points each)</p>

<p class="en-question"><strong>A1. According to the text, the thieves…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) pretended to be visitors of the museum.</span>
  <span>(b) recut the jewels they took from the cases.</span>
  <span>(c) picked items randomly.</span>
  <span>(d) failed to escape with all the jewels.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(d) failed to escape with all the jewels.</strong></p>
  <p>Durante la huida se les cayó una de las piezas, la corona de la emperatriz Eugenia (líneas 9-10): <em>“During the escape, there was a moment of carelessness: they dropped Empress Eugénie’s exquisite 19th-century crown.”</em></p>
  <p>Las demás opciones no encajan: entraron por un balcón con una plataforma elevadora, no haciéndose pasar por visitantes (a); el texto solo dice que los diamantes <em>pueden</em> retallarse (<em>“can be recut”</em>), no que ellos lo hicieran (b); y sabían exactamente lo que buscaban (<em>“They knew exactly what they were after”</em>), así que no eligieron al azar (c).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-question"><strong>A2. According to the text, the thief who stole the <em>Mona Lisa</em>…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) acted in broad daylight.</span>
  <span>(b) was the Louvre’s first major thief.</span>
  <span>(c) made a mistake while leaving the museum.</span>
  <span>(d) was a professional criminal.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(b) was the Louvre’s first major thief.</strong></p>
  <p>El robo de la <em>Mona Lisa</em> en 1911 es el único precedente real de un gran robo en el Louvre (líneas 17-18): <em>“the only real precedent was the notorious 1911 disappearance of Leonardo da Vinci’s La Joconde”</em>.</p>
  <p>No actuó a plena luz del día, sino que se escondió durante la noche (<em>“hid in a closet overnight”</em>) (a); salió sin más (<em>“simply walked out”</em>), sin cometer ningún error (c); y no era un delincuente profesional, sino un nacionalista italiano (<em>“an Italian nationalist”</em>) (d).</p>
</Solucion>
