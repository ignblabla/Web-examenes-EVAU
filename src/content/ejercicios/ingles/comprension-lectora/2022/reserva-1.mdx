<p class="en-heading">TEXT 1: A PIZZA FOR THE QUEEN</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">Pizza Margherita, whose toppings resemble the colours of the Italian flag, red (tomato), white (mozzarella) and green (basil),</li>
  <li>has an appealing story behind its name. It was created by Raffaele Esposito, a well-known pizza maker from Naples who owned</li>
  <li>a tavern called <em>Pizzeria di Pietro e Basta Cosí</em>. Tomato-based pizza was then considered the food of the poor people of Naples</li>
  <li>and mainly sold by the street vendors. In his book <em>Le Coricollo</em>, French author Alexandre Dumas noted the popularity of the tomato-</li>
  <li>and-fish-based pizzas sold in the streets of Naples. People would eat watermelons in summer and pizza throughout the year.</li>
  <li class="is-start">According to the legend, Esposito was invited to the royal palace to prepare a pizza for Queen Margherita of Savoy, who had</li>
  <li>travelled to Naples with King Umberto I. Esposito prepared three different pizzas, one of which used a combination of tomatoes,</li>
  <li>mozzarella cheese and basil to emulate the red, white and green of the Italian flag. The Queen found this pizza absolutely delicious</li>
  <li>and she is said to have sent a note of appreciation to the pizza maker, who then named the pizza after the Queen.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Although the authenticity of the story has been questioned by some people, it helped Neapolitan Pizza gain enduring popularity</li>
  <li>and the presentation and fine taste of Esposito’s Pizza Margherita made it one of the most recognizable symbols of Italian food</li>
  <li>culture in the world. Some of the old traditional pizzerias still exist in Naples, and a trip to this capital city of the Campania region</li>
  <li>must certainly include a visit to its historic pizzerias for both pizza and history lovers.</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-task">CHOOSE AND WRITE THE CORRECT OPTION (A, B, C or D). (0.5 points each)</p>

<p class="en-question"><strong>1. According to the text, Alexandre Dumas wrote about…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) the street-sold pizzas in one of his books.</span>
  <span>(b) Raffaele Esposito.</span>
  <span>(c) the popularity of Queen Margherita.</span>
  <span>(d) how to make tomato and fish pizza.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(a) the street-sold pizzas in one of his books.</strong></p>
  <p>En uno de sus libros habló de las pizzas que se vendían por la calle (líneas 4-5): <em>“In his book Le Coricollo, French author Alexandre Dumas noted the popularity of the tomato-and-fish-based pizzas sold in the streets of Naples.”</em></p>
  <p>El texto no dice que escribiera sobre Esposito (b) ni sobre la reina (c); y habló de lo populares que eran esas pizzas, no de cómo se preparaban (d).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-question"><strong>2. After Esposito made pizza for the Queen, …</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) he became the royal cook.</span>
  <span>(b) he opened a tavern.</span>
  <span>(c) he opened a lot of pizzerias in Naples.</span>
  <span>(d) one of his recipes became well-known all over the world.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(d) one of his recipes became well-known all over the world.</strong></p>
  <p>Su pizza Margherita se convirtió en un símbolo de la cocina italiana en todo el mundo (líneas 11-12): <em>“made it one of the most recognizable symbols of Italian food culture in the world.”</em></p>
  <p>El texto no dice que se hiciera cocinero real (a); ya tenía una taberna antes (b); y no habla de que abriera más pizzerías (c).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-heading en-gap">TEXT 2: WILL THE MACHINES TAKE CONTROL?</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">The first time I interviewed Eric Schmidt —twelve years ago when he was the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) of Google— I had</li>
  <li>a simple question about the technology that is spying and monetizing all our movements, opinions, relationships and tastes. “Friend</li>
  <li>or enemy?” I asked. “We’re friends,” Schmidt replied coolly.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Now that the former Google executive has a book out titled <em>The Age of Artificial Intelligence</em>, I made him the same question</li>
  <li>about Artificial Intelligence (AI), “Friend or enemy?”. “AI is imprecise, and it can be unreliable as a partner,” he replied. “It’s dynamic</li>
  <li>since it’s changing all the time. It’s emergent and does things you don’t expect. And, most importantly, it’s capable of learning.”</li>
  <li class="is-start">“It will be everywhere,” continues Schmidt. “How may a child feel about having an AI programmed robot? What does AI war</li>
  <li>look like? Does AI perceive aspects of reality that we don’t? Is it possible that AI will see things that humans cannot comprehend?”</li>
  <li class="is-start">Some scientists believe that when we build AI without a kill bottom for it, we are creating a demon —with luck, humans could</li>
  <li>end up as the family pets. On this point, Schmidt disagrees with those people who consider that by developing AI, eventually, you’ll</li>
  <li>finish up with a master robot that’s 1,000 times smarter than the humans. “My answer is different. I think that these AI systems are</li>
  <li>going to think, not like humans, but they’re going to be very smart, and that we’re going to have to coexist.”</li>
  <li class="is-start">I asked, “You don’t think Siri and Alexa are going to kill us one night?”. “No,” he replied. “But they might become your child’s</li>
  <li>best friend.”</li>
</ol>
