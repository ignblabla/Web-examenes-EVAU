<p class="en-heading">TEXT 1: SAVING ABU SIMBEL</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">In the early 1960s, Egypt had to improve agriculture and provide electricity for its increasing population. In order to do so, the</li>
  <li>country planned to build a huge dam across the Nile River that would flood an area with important archaeological sites. In the eyes</li>
  <li>of the Egyptian government, the destruction of the treasures, while regrettable, was necessary. Christiane Desroches-Noblecourt,</li>
  <li>the female chief curator of Egyptian antiquities at the Louvre Museum, appealed to Egyptian officials not to resign themselves to</li>
  <li>this catastrophic loss of their cultural heritage.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Christiane proposed to them the most difficult archaeological rescue in history —a project of almost unthinkable magnitude and</li>
  <li>complexity consisting in moving the fragile sandstone temple of Abu Simbel to higher ground. On top of the enormous engineering</li>
  <li>problems, the project required cooperation among countries at a time of escalating global political tensions. But that didn’t stop her.</li>
  <li class="is-start">After months of countless meetings, Christiane eventually won the support of UNESCO and Egypt’s Minister of Culture, who</li>
  <li>persuaded the Egyptian president to approve the rescue plan. This was a major step, but the project was doomed to fail without</li>
  <li>massive financial help from Western countries, particularly the United States. It was then that an unexpected saviour, Jacqueline</li>
  <li>Kennedy, appeared on the scene. Thanks to her influence, her husband, President John F. Kennedy, called on US Congress to</li>
  <li>authorise money to ensure the rescue.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Ironically, the two women who played crucial roles in this landmark rescue apparently had no idea of each other’s moves. Both</li>
  <li>Christiane and Jacqueline had worked behind the scenes. Neither of them ever sought or received public attention for their</li>
  <li>achievement, caring less about the credit than getting the job done.</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-task">CHOOSE AND WRITE THE CORRECT OPTION (A, B, C or D). (0.5 points each)</p>

<p class="en-question"><strong>1. According to the text, Abu Simbel…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) was buried under the sand.</span>
  <span>(b) was damaged during the rescue.</span>
  <span>(c) went through a complex relocation.</span>
  <span>(d) was moved abroad.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(c) went through a complex relocation.</strong></p>
  <p>El templo se trasladó a un terreno más alto, en un proyecto de una complejidad casi inimaginable (líneas 6-7): <em>“a project of almost unthinkable magnitude and complexity consisting in moving the fragile sandstone temple of Abu Simbel to higher ground.”</em></p>
  <p>La amenaza era el agua de la presa, no la arena (a); el texto no habla de daños (b); y se movió a un terreno más alto en Egipto, no al extranjero (d).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-question"><strong>2. According to the text, Jacqueline Kennedy…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) negotiated with UNESCO.</span>
  <span>(b) convinced Christiane to save the temple.</span>
  <span>(c) tried to acquire the temple.</span>
  <span>(d) helped get the project financed.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(d) helped get the project financed.</strong></p>
  <p>Gracias a ella, su marido pidió al Congreso que aprobara el dinero para el rescate (líneas 12-13): <em>“Thanks to her influence, her husband, President John F. Kennedy, called on US Congress to authorise money to ensure the rescue.”</em></p>
  <p>Quien consiguió el apoyo de la UNESCO fue Christiane (a); ninguna de las dos sabía lo que hacía la otra (b); y el texto no dice que intentara comprar el templo (c).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-heading en-gap">TEXT 2: THE STRENGTH OF WEAK SOCIAL TIES</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">I’ve been working as a psychotherapist since 1988. Through the years, I’ve dealt with hundreds of patients who come to my</li>
  <li>practice because they feel lonely. Many report finding relief in brief, casual encounters. That’s the case of Alice, who lived alone</li>
  <li>for years after a divorce. Her family lived away from her, and she described feeling terribly lonesome, especially during the holidays.</li>
  <li>One day, I asked her what helped cheer her up. “I love going to the library and chatting with Maria, the librarian, about historical</li>
  <li>novels or cozy mysteries. I always leave feeling a warm connection with her.”</li>
  <li class="is-start">Another patient, Sherry, gets great joy from her Sunday breakfasts at a local diner: “The manager’s so nice. She calls me</li>
  <li>‘Sweety’ and brings me extra whipped cream for my pancakes. I feel uplifted.”</li>
  <li class="is-start">As for me, when I shop for groceries, I always go to a cashier I’ve chatted with for years. Our shared kindness and familiarity</li>
  <li>give me a sense of community. What’s more, even those we meet only once can leave a lasting impression. I’ll never forget</li>
  <li>discussing <em>Don Quixote</em> on an airplane with a well-read economics professor.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Relationships like these, generally referred to as “weak social ties”, have been proved to be just as important to life satisfaction</li>
  <li>as core or strong ties. When we feel blue or lonely, we tend to turn down social engagements, usually to avoid the imagined</li>
  <li>embarrassment of being the only sad person in a group. But saying yes, despite the hesitation, offers an opportunity to feel less</li>
  <li>isolated. Staying mindful of our strong and weak ties allows us to benefit from the comfort and the sense of community they provide.</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-task">CHOOSE AND WRITE THE CORRECT OPTION (A, B, C or D). (0.5 points each)</p>

<p class="en-question"><strong>9. Alice…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) has been a client of the author’s since 1988.</span>
  <span>(b) feels all alone when her family goes away on vacation.</span>
  <span>(c) and her therapist go to the same library.</span>
  <span>(d) enjoys casual conversation about shared interests.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(d) enjoys casual conversation about shared interests.</strong></p>
  <p>Le gusta charlar con la bibliotecaria sobre los libros que les gustan a las dos (líneas 4-5): <em>“I love going to the library and chatting with Maria, the librarian, about historical novels or cozy mysteries.”</em></p>
  <p>1988 es el año en que la autora empezó a trabajar, no cuando Alice se hizo paciente (a); su familia vive lejos y se siente sola en vacaciones, no cuando ellos se van de viaje (b); y el texto no dice que la terapeuta vaya a esa biblioteca (c).</p>
</Solucion>

</Solucion>
