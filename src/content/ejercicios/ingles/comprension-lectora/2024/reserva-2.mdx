<p class="en-heading">TEXT 1: THE 30-MILLION-DOLLAR SANDWICH</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">During the sixties, the United States was locked in a race with the Soviet Union for dominance in space. The question became</li>
  <li>who would get to the Moon first. In March 1965, NASA launched the spaceship Gemini 3 to develop space travel techniques in</li>
  <li>weightless conditions. It was the first U.S. mission to carry two astronauts —John Young and his crewmate, Gus Grissom.</li>
  <li class="is-start">As the frozen meals that NASA had selected were not exactly appetizing to Young’s palate, he slipped a beef sandwich into</li>
  <li>his pocket just before the spaceship was launched. Two days later at lunchtime, Young shared a bite of his hidden sandwich with</li>
  <li>Grissom. Suddenly, breadcrumbs started floating around due to the lack of gravity, putting at risk the pieces of equipment that kept</li>
  <li>the 30-million spacecraft running. Aware of the serious threat, the astronauts opted to put the sandwich away and continued their</li>
  <li>mission with crumbs floating around the cabin.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Young’s special menu didn’t cause any harm but it became a bigger issue back on Earth, resulting in an official review to</li>
  <li>discuss the incident. Young was given an official reprimand, the first ever for a NASA member. He eventually regretted taking the</li>
  <li>sandwich to space, but he later wrote that he didn’t think it was such a big deal. Firstly, because to his knowledge, it wasn’t even</li>
  <li>the first “illegal” sandwich but the third. And secondly, he joked, it didn’t even have mustard!</li>
  <li class="is-start">While the infamous sandwich is no longer available, a similar one, preserved in acrylic, is on display at the Grissom Memorial</li>
  <li>Museum. It immortalizes the historic moment when an astronaut dared to eat a beef sandwich in space.</li>
</ol>

</Solucion>

<p class="en-heading en-gap">TEXT 2: YOUR NOSE KNOWS</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">Smell is a powerful sense. Why can a particular smell take us immediately to a vivid moment of our past? Smell and memory</li>
  <li>are so closely linked because, according to Harvard professor Venkatesh Murthy, “olfactory signals have direct access to the limbic</li>
  <li>system —the brain centre of emotion and memory.” Smell also plays a huge role in taste. When you chew, food molecules “make</li>
  <li>their way back to your nasal epithelium, so what you consider flavour is really smell.” Murthy says you can check this by pinching</li>
  <li>your nose when eating chocolate. With your nose passageway blocked, you’ll just taste sweetness instead of chocolate’s distinctive</li>
  <li>flavour.</li>
  <li class="is-start">History confirms the importance of smell. Hieroglyphics show Ancient Egyptians making perfume as long ago as 3,000 BC.</li>
  <li>Egyptian priests burnt aromatic resins to sweeten the air in holy ceremonials and burials because they believed that this ritual</li>
  <li>connected humans with the gods. When those tombs were opened in the 1800s, the perfumes still maintained their original smells.</li>
  <li>There are also references to perfume in the Bible, and Chinese writings on aromatic products date even further back. So, when</li>
  <li>was perfumery born? Well, Stone Age humans had fire and could burn fragrant woods, but until archaeologists come across new</li>
  <li>evidence, we can only guess.</li>
  <li class="is-start">In 16<sup>th</sup>- and 17<sup>th</sup>-century Europe, people associated unpleasant smells with disease. Wealthy people used scented things like</li>
  <li>cosmetics or perfumed gloves in the hope that their pleasant aroma could protect them against infection.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Unsurprisingly, today’s perfume industry is a multi-million market. The latest thing: exclusive stores and hotels which have their</li>
  <li>own signature scents to make a lasting impression on customers through the power of perfume.</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-task">CHOOSE AND WRITE THE CORRECT OPTION (A, B, C or D). (0.5 points each)</p>

<p class="en-question"><strong>9. According to the text, the use of perfumed substances in Ancient Egypt…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) is mentioned in the Bible.</span>
  <span>(b) was meant to mask the smell of death.</span>
  <span>(c) played a religious role.</span>
  <span>(d) has not been documented.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(c) played a religious role.</strong></p>
  <p>Los sacerdotes quemaban resinas aromáticas en ceremonias sagradas porque creían que unían a los humanos con los dioses (líneas 8-9): <em>“Egyptian priests burnt aromatic resins to sweeten the air in holy ceremonials and burials because they believed that this ritual connected humans with the gods.”</em></p>
  <p>La Biblia habla del perfume en general, no del uso egipcio (a); el motivo era religioso, no ocultar el olor de la muerte (b); y los jeroglíficos lo documentan (d).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-question"><strong>10. Historically, perfume was once believed to…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) be a gift from the gods.</span>
  <span>(b) intensify body smell.</span>
  <span>(c) affect people’s emotions.</span>
  <span>(d) prevent illness.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(d) prevent illness.</strong></p>
  <p>En los siglos XVI y XVII se pensaba que los buenos olores protegían de las infecciones (línea 14): <em>“in the hope that their pleasant aroma could protect them against infection.”</em></p>
  <p>El texto no dice que se considerara un regalo de los dioses (a), ni que intensificara el olor corporal (b); y la relación con las emociones es un dato científico actual, no una creencia histórica sobre el perfume (c).</p>
</Solucion>
