<p class="en-heading">TEXT 1: YOUNG PEOPLE’S SCREEN TIME</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">As we can attest from firsthand experience, regular TV viewership has been decreasing as streaming services like Netflix are</li>
  <li>becoming increasingly popular. The amount of time kids and teens spend watching TV has dropped. In 2013, young people spent</li>
  <li>an average of 3 hours per day in front of the television screen; today, it’s an hour and 12 minutes. So, have youth reduced their</li>
  <li>screen time? Not really. Studies show that they have rather traded the TV screen for their phone and laptop. Now the average</li>
  <li>youth watches 97 minutes of YouTube and 95 minutes of TikTok per day.</li>
  <li class="is-start">A few years ago, two Oxford researchers captured the media’s attention by concluding that screen time was as bad for teen</li>
  <li>mental health as… potatoes! Their paper even stated that social media had no psychological negative effects. However, most</li>
  <li>evidence points to the contrary. Two Spanish researchers argued that the Oxford study’s conclusions were “extremely misleading”,</li>
  <li>after they found that the use of social media was strongly linked to mental health issues, while TV and gaming were only weakly</li>
  <li>related. Other studies confirmed this and added that the worst effects impacted girls 50% more than boys.</li>
  <li class="is-start">It’s time to abandon the idea that screen time doesn’t matter for mental health. It does. It’s just that some screen time matters</li>
  <li>more than others. Social media seems the most problematic. For practical purposes, that’s good news. Parents, policymakers,</li>
  <li>and teens themselves don’t need to consider giving up technology; instead, they can focus on cutting back on social media time.</li>
  <li>There are other ways teens can stay in touch with friends virtually, like texting, gaming together or meeting on FaceTime or Zoom.</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-task">CHOOSE AND WRITE THE CORRECT OPTION (A, B, C or D). (0.5 points each)</p>

<p class="en-question"><strong>1. According to the text, young people today…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) watch Netflix more than other age groups.</span>
  <span>(b) use multiple devices for entertainment.</span>
  <span>(c) prefer regular television to YouTube.</span>
  <span>(d) only watch content online.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(b) use multiple devices for entertainment.</strong></p>
  <p>Los jóvenes siguen viendo la televisión, aunque menos, y además usan el móvil y el portátil (línea 4): <em>“they have rather traded the TV screen for their phone and laptop”</em>, con YouTube y TikTok cada día.</p>
  <p>El texto no compara a los jóvenes con otros grupos de edad (a); ven más YouTube (97 minutos) que televisión (una hora y 12 minutos) (c); y siguen viendo algo de televisión convencional, así que no lo ven todo por internet (d).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-question"><strong>2. The Oxford research on social media…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) was contradicted by several researchers.</span>
  <span>(b) showed it harmed girls more than boys.</span>
  <span>(c) is supported by extensive data.</span>
  <span>(d) went unnoticed in the press.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(a) was contradicted by several researchers.</strong></p>
  <p>Dos investigadores españoles calificaron sus conclusiones de engañosas, y otros estudios les dieron la razón (líneas 8-10): <em>“Two Spanish researchers argued that the Oxford study’s conclusions were ‘extremely misleading’… Other studies confirmed this”</em>.</p>
  <p>Fueron otros estudios, no el de Oxford, los que mostraron que afecta más a las chicas (b); la mayoría de los datos dicen lo contrario (<em>“most evidence points to the contrary”</em>) (c); y llamó la atención de los medios (<em>“captured the media’s attention”</em>) (d).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-heading en-gap">TEXT 2: CHARLES DICKENS</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">Charles Dickens is one of the greatest writers in English literature. His stories such as <em>Great Expectations</em> and <em>Oliver Twist</em></li>
  <li>continue to shape the way in which we understand nineteenth-century England.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Forget the idea that Dickens was always a rich, benevolent gentleman. His family moved around a lot in Dickens’s youth, from</li>
  <li>Portsmouth to Kent, and from there to London. Dickens’s father was getting deeper and deeper into debt, and the family were</li>
  <li>literally running away from their creditors. When he was 12, Dickens was sent to live with a family friend and to work at Warren’s</li>
  <li>shoe polish factory. In his youth, Dickens thought of becoming an actor. However, he fell in love with a woman whose parents were</li>
  <li>middle-class and disapproved of her association with an aspiring actor —actors being seen as of a low social status at the time.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Once he was a famous writer, Dickens became a well-known philanthropist who committed himself to many charities,</li>
  <li>particularly focusing on issues of child poverty and education. On the same altruistic note, Dickens was also the editor of magazines</li>
  <li>that, at popular prices, brought literature to everybody. They featured stories by different writers, established novelists, including</li>
  <li>himself, and also women, like the first salaried female journalist, Eliza Lynn Linton.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Dickens wrote <em>A Christmas Carol</em> rapidly, taking just six weeks from beginning to end. He was forced to pay half the publication</li>
  <li>costs himself as his publishers failed to see the value of a Christmas story. Ironically, it’s never been out of print since its publication</li>
  <li>in 1843 and it’s been considered one of Dickens’s finest literary achievements, as well as an inspiration for numerous films.</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-question"><strong>9. As an editor, Charles Dickens…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) published some stories written by women.</span>
  <span>(b) focused on promising new writers.</span>
  <span>(c) was seeking to make a lot of money.</span>
  <span>(d) published stories about working women.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(a) published some stories written by women.</strong></p>
  <p>Sus revistas incluían relatos de distintos autores, también de mujeres (líneas 10-11): <em>“They featured stories by different writers, established novelists, including himself, and also women, like the first salaried female journalist, Eliza Lynn Linton.”</em></p>
  <p>Publicaba a novelistas ya consagrados (<em>“established novelists”</em>), no se centraba en nuevas promesas (b); vendía las revistas a precios populares para acercar la literatura a todos, con un fin altruista (<em>“at popular prices, brought literature to everybody”</em>) (c); y las mujeres eran autoras de los relatos, no su tema (d).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-question"><strong>10. According to the text, <em>A Christmas Carol</em>…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) was seen as a great story by publishers.</span>
  <span>(b) was quite hard to write.</span>
  <span>(c) had to be co-financed by Dickens himself.</span>
  <span>(d) is claimed to be a minor work.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(c) had to be co-financed by Dickens himself.</strong></p>
  <p>Dickens tuvo que pagar de su bolsillo la mitad de los costes de publicación (líneas 12-13): <em>“He was forced to pay half the publication costs himself”</em>.</p>
  <p>Los editores no vieron el valor de la historia (<em>“his publishers failed to see the value of a Christmas story”</em>) (a); la escribió deprisa, en solo seis semanas (<em>“rapidly, taking just six weeks”</em>) (b); y se considera una de sus mejores obras (<em>“one of Dickens’s finest literary achievements”</em>) (d).</p>
</Solucion>
