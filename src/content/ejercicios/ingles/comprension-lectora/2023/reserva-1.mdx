<p class="en-heading">TEXT 1: ANIME: A GLOBAL POP CULTURE PHENOMENON</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">The origin of anime can be traced back to the beginning of the 20th century. However, it wasn’t until 1958 that Hiroshi Okawa,</li>
  <li>president of the Toei Doga production company, decided to turn it into the “Disney of the East.” His efforts were worth it, and anime</li>
  <li>gradually became a global cultural phenomenon. In order to understand how significant this phenomenon is we can travel back to</li>
  <li>2004, when Japan sent trucks to Iraq to help reconstruction. The Japanese feared that the local population would not recognize</li>
  <li>their flag stamped on the trucks’ sides. To avoid this, they decorated the trucks with posters of Captain Tsubasa, a popular anime</li>
  <li>character.</li>
  <li class="is-start">One reason why anime gained such popularity internationally in the 90s was its distinctive style, easily recognizable by its</li>
  <li>simplified animation and its characters’ disproportionately big eyes. The animation style was basic because reducing the number</li>
  <li>of frames helped to save money. The second trait, the huge eyes, responds to multiple factors. Big eyes make it easier for</li>
  <li>animators to express emotions powerfully by concentrating on just one body part. At the same time, audiences are positively drawn</li>
  <li>to big round eyes, which make characters look innocent and young. Japanese culture also plays a part in anime’s expressive eyes,</li>
  <li>since the polite thing in Japan is not to show emotions openly through body language but through subtle eye expressions.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Anime has stayed hugely successful worldwide because it appeals to a wide range of people of all ages and nationalities who</li>
  <li>keep loyal to their cartoons for years. The emotional soundtracks and eye-catching colours keep hooking fans today.</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-task">CHOOSE AND WRITE THE CORRECT OPTION (A, B, C or D). (0.5 points each)</p>

<p class="en-question"><strong>1. According to the text, Hiroshi Okawa…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) founded Toei Doga studios.</span>
  <span>(b) designed the first anime characters.</span>
  <span>(c) had worked for Disney studios.</span>
  <span>(d) made anime internationally known.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(d) made anime internationally known.</strong></p>
  <p>Quiso convertir el anime en “la Disney de Oriente” y lo consiguió: se convirtió en un fenómeno mundial (líneas 1-3), <em>“His efforts were worth it, and anime gradually became a global cultural phenomenon.”</em></p>
  <p>Era el presidente de la productora, no su fundador (a); el anime existía desde principios del siglo XX (b); y el texto no dice que trabajara para Disney (c).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-question"><strong>2. According to the text, the Japanese used Captain Tsubasa in order to…</strong></p>

<div class="en-options">
  <span>(a) frighten the Iraqi armed forces.</span>
  <span>(b) create a friendlier Japanese flag.</span>
  <span>(c) help Iraqis to identify Japanese aid.</span>
  <span>(d) promote anime culture in Iraq.</span>
</div>

<Solucion>
  <p><strong>(c) help Iraqis to identify Japanese aid.</strong></p>
  <p>Temían que los iraquíes no reconocieran su bandera, así que decoraron los camiones con el personaje (líneas 4-5): <em>“The Japanese feared that the local population would not recognize their flag… To avoid this, they decorated the trucks with posters of Captain Tsubasa”</em>.</p>
  <p>Los camiones iban a ayudar en la reconstrucción, no a asustar a nadie (a); la bandera no cambió, se añadieron carteles (b); y el objetivo era que se reconociera la ayuda, no promocionar el anime (d).</p>
</Solucion>

<p class="en-heading en-gap">TEXT 2: THIS JOB IS A PIECE OF CAKE!</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">It turns out you might be able to eat candy for a living and make thousands in the process. Canada’s Candy Funhouse is hiring</li>
  <li>a chief candy officer, according to a recent job posting.</li>
  <li class="is-start">“Do you love candy and chocolate? Are you passionate about sweet treats and exploring unreleased and existing products? If</li>
  <li>so, this is the perfect position for you!” the description reads. The Ontario-based company says the position pays 100,000 Canadian</li>
  <li>dollars annually. The job can be remote or based in its Canada or New Jersey offices.</li>
  <li class="is-start">The chief candy officer will serve as head taste tester and try over 3,500 products monthly. Besides eating about 113 pieces</li>
  <li>of candy a day, the chief candy officer will lead the company’s candy strategy, run candy board meetings and have a say in which</li>
  <li>products Candy Funhouse will release into the market. Applicants can be as young as 5 years old and must reside in North</li>
  <li>America.</li>
  <li class="is-start">“All you need is a passion for candy and pop culture, and obviously a sweet tooth!” According to the job description, the chief</li>
  <li>candy officer will also undergo “extensive palate training,” which will be provided by the company. No previous experience is</li>
  <li>necessary. So far, nearly 6,500 people have applied for the job. Inquiries on the job are so numerous that the company has</li>
  <li>admitted that its usual response time is delayed.</li>
  <li class="is-start">The position no doubt involves excessive consumption of sugar, which the World Health Organization recommends limiting to</li>
  <li>10% of the daily calorie intake. Too much sugar can increase the risk of Type-2 diabetes and heart disease. At least, the job</li>
  <li>posting specifies that the position comes with an “extensive dental plan.”</li>
</ol>
