<p class="en-heading">SPOGOMI</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start"><em>SpoGomi</em> is a combination of two words, <em>sport</em> and <em>gomi</em>, the Japanese word for rubbish. Invented in 2008, SpoGomi was</li>
  <li>designed as a competitive activity to encourage people to clean up public spaces. It has since been introduced in Japanese</li>
  <li>schools, where students now collect litter while having fun.</li>
  <li class="is-start">According to the rules, teams of three players have 60 minutes to collect as much litter as possible from a designated area.</li>
  <li>The waste must be sorted into color-coded bags according to type, such as burnable waste, recyclable plastic, metal cans, and</li>
  <li>more. At the end of the competition, the collected litter is weighed and checked for correct sorting, and the team with the highest</li>
  <li>score wins. In the event of a tie, the winner is determined by the “quality” of the rubbish collected, with cigarette butts earning the</li>
  <li>most points.</li>
  <li class="is-start">The public-spirited sport of SpoGomi quickly gained popularity in Britain. In a surprising outcome, the British team beat the</li>
  <li>Japanese in the Spogomi World Cup celebrated in Tokyo in 2023. One of the winning competitors was Sarah Parry, a 29-year-old</li>
  <li>doctor from Glasgow, who—together with her two teammates—collected 28 kilograms more litter than the host nation.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Parry describes herself as highly competitive. After her brother came across an advertisement for SpoGomi, she assembled a</li>
  <li>team and signed up for the World Cup. “We were not avid litter-pickers in our free time,” she explains. “It was luck, competitiveness</li>
  <li>and enthusiasm more than anything else.”</li>
  <li class="is-start">Although litter-picking may sound calm, Parry emphasizes that it is physically demanding. “It’s very difficult,” she says. “I have</li>
  <li>run 33 marathons, and the sorest my legs have ever been was after winning the SpoGomi World Cup.” She explains that</li>
  <li>competitors must walk quickly while carrying a lot of awkwardly shaped items, sometimes collecting more than 50 kilograms of</li>
  <li>waste in a busy urban environment. This involves using very different muscle groups.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Parry believes SpoGomi is unlike any other sport: “It is very fun. You don’t often get the chance to play sport in a busy urban</li>
  <li>area where people don’t understand what you are doing and why you are so excited about spotting a glass bottle.” More importantly,</li>
  <li>she adds, SpoGomi has drawn attention to a very important issue: the litter problem affecting Britain’s streets. Although the sport</li>
  <li>originated in Japan, the country has far less litter than the UK. Parry hopes SpoGomi will become massively popular in her country</li>
  <li>and inspire people to care more about waste. “I wasn’t particularly eco-minded myself, and SpoGomi completely changed my</li>
  <li>perspective,” she notes.</li>
  <li class="is-start">SpoGomi is not really about rewards. Instead, participants of all ages enjoy the competitive nature of the sport, the teamwork</li>
  <li>that it encourages, the valuable time spent outdoors and, last but certainly not least, the opportunity to keep their cities clean.</li>
  <li>“That’s what’s really nice about the sport,” Parry concludes.</li>
</ol>
