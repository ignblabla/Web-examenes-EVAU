<p class="en-heading">A FLASH CHINESE SPEAKER</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">Australian Ben McMahon had learned some basic Chinese in high school but was far from fluent. In 2015, a semi-trailer truck</li>
  <li>ran a red light and smashed into the side of the car he was driving. The crash fractured his sternum and several ribs, left him with</li>
  <li>a severe head trauma, and put him into an induced coma for a week. When Ben finally regained consciousness, waking was a</li>
  <li>fog. For two weeks he moved in and out of consciousness. When he finally began to speak, the nurse attending him was astonished</li>
  <li>and rushed to tell his parents that their son was awake, though speaking a language she barely understood. Ben’s parents were</li>
  <li>filled with joy that he was alive, though shocked at the change, because he was speaking fluently—but in Chinese.</li>
  <li class="is-start">At first Ben had no idea that anything was unusual. Every time he spoke, Chinese flowed naturally from his mouth. Before the</li>
  <li>accident, he had managed only basic exchanges, but now his internal monologue—the voice in his head—had switched languages</li>
  <li>completely. There was no translation from English, just effortless speech, as if Chinese had always been his mother tongue.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Neurologists later speculated that the trauma to Ben’s brain might have altered how his language centers worked. For most</li>
  <li>English speakers, language processing occurs largely in the brain’s left hemisphere; Chinese, however, tends to engage both</li>
  <li>hemispheres more equally. Because the left side of Ben’s brain had sustained the most damage, doctors theorized that his brain</li>
  <li>had temporarily relocated linguistic processing to the right, making Chinese the more accessible language.</li>
  <li class="is-start">It took about a week for Ben to start distinguishing between English and Chinese again. He gradually began to speak English</li>
  <li>with his parents and brother but could still use Chinese with hospital staff. Within a month he was home, recovering—and amazed</li>
  <li>at how his life had changed.</li>
  <li class="is-start">In time, his fluency opened unexpected doors. One led him to appear on If You Are the One, China’s most-watched dating</li>
  <li>show with an audience of eighty million. Onstage, twenty-four women quizzed him about his background, job, and hobbies. Ben</li>
  <li>tried to charm them by handing out Australian cookies and playing a Chinese song on the mandolin. One contestant gave him a</li>
  <li>“super-like,” and though she and Ben dated briefly, they soon realized they weren’t a match.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Still, Ben saw the experience as another gift of fate. He often spoke of <em>Mìngyùn</em>, the Chinese notion of destiny that connects</li>
  <li>people and paths. He believed it was fate that had tied him to China and to the language that had, quite literally, saved his voice.</li>
</ol>
