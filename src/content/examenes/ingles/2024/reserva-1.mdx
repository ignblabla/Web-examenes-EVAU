<p class="en-heading">TEXT 1: LA CATRINA</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">Mexicans have an exceptional relationship with death and the afterlife because they are quite unique in taking it good-</li>
  <li>humoredly and passionately. That doesn’t mean that Mexicans don’t grieve or miss those who have passed away —they just</li>
  <li>choose to remember them by celebrating their lives and memories. On November 2nd, the Day of the Dead, families gather in</li>
  <li>cemeteries to feast while they clean tombstones, sing songs, and talk to their ancestors. Parents might even introduce a baby to</li>
  <li>a grandparent who died before the baby was born.</li>
  <li class="is-start">One of the most recognizable symbols of the Day of the Dead celebrations is La Catrina, a female skeleton wearing a fancy</li>
  <li>hat. Her striking makeup has become trendy worldwide. The essence of La Catrina’s story goes deep into Mexican roots as the</li>
  <li>Aztecs worshipped Mictecacihuatl, a goddess of death that protected and helped their departed loved ones into the next stages.</li>
  <li>However, the skull that we see today was created in the early 1900s by José Guadalupe Posada. This cartoonist drew <em>calaveras</em></li>
  <li>in a satirical way to urge people to stop pretending to be something that they weren’t, for they would all die someday.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Later, famous artist and husband of Frida Kahlo, Diego Rivera, immortalized Posada’s Catrina in one of his murals. He painted</li>
  <li>her wearing sophisticated clothing and an extravagant hat with feathers, thus creating the multicolored look that she’s well-known</li>
  <li>for today. From there, La Catrina became a powerful symbol for the numerous Day of the Dead events. Women use colorful</li>
  <li>makeup and wear lively outfits evoking the famous skeleton.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Tourists find La Catrina fascinating and many purchase Catrina figurines as souvenirs. La Catrina is definitely a Mexican icon!</li>
</ol>

<p class="en-heading en-gap">TEXT 2: THE DISAPPEARANCE OF MICHAEL ROCKEFELLER</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">Michael Rockefeller, great-grandson of the founder of the wealthy Rockefeller dynasty, was deeply impressed when his father</li>
  <li>opened the Museum of Primitive Art in 1960. He decided there and then to devote his life to finding “primitive” art —a term no</li>
  <li>longer used today due to its implications that non-Western cultures are inferior. Within months of the museum launch, Michael</li>
  <li>went on an expedition trip to New Guinea —ruled by the Netherlands at the time— to collect art from the native Asmat people.</li>
  <li>Michael learned on arrival that Asmat warriors took the heads of their enemies in battle and that they practised cannibalism. He</li>
  <li>was fascinated by these people, who represented such an intriguing violation of the norms of Western society.</li>
  <li class="is-start">When Michael returned to the island one year later, his boat sank. The Rockefellers spent a small fortune supporting the</li>

  <li>never seen again. The official cause of death was filed as drowning, but the news and rumours about it spread like wildfire in</li>
  <li>tabloids and newspapers.</li>
  <li class="is-start">In 2014, reporter Carl Hoffman revealed in a book that the Netherlands’ inquiries into the matter had concluded, shortly after</li>
