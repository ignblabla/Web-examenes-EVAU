<p class="en-heading">THE INGENIOUS STORY BEHIND MICHELIN STARS</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">If you made a reservation at a Michelin-starred restaurant, you might wonder why an elite restaurant guide has the same name</li>
  <li>as Michelin, a company that makes tires. The roots of what became an incredibly influential ranking system did not begin with the</li>
  <li>intent of recommending the highest-quality restaurants. Actually, it was a clever publicity campaign now legendary for its success.</li>
  <li class="is-start">The origin of the Michelin Star goes back to the early days of the automobile. French brothers André and Édouard Michelin</li>
  <li>founded their self-named tire company in 1889. It was a pioneering move considering that there were fewer than 3,000 automobiles</li>
  <li>in France at the time. Soon the brothers realized that if people drove more, the tires would eventually deteriorate, which would, as</li>
  <li>a result, increase Michelin’s sales. So, much like bloggers do today, they launched the Michelin Guide in 1900 to achieve that goal.</li>
  <li class="is-start">The guide contained information that any motorist would need—a detailed map, a compendium of mechanics and fuel stations,</li>
  <li>instructions on how to replace a flat tire, and suitable restaurants and hotels to eat and stay while journeying across France. There</li>
  <li>was nothing quite like that in the market, so it became an instant hit.</li>
  <li class="is-start">In 1926, the Michelins introduced the concept of using a Michelin Star to rank restaurants. Most of this information came from</li>
  <li>the company’s traveling salesmen, who spent much of their time on the roads and were, therefore, highly reliable sources. By the</li>
  <li>late 1920s, Michelin’s restaurant recommendations had become so influential that the brothers started to use undercover</li>
  <li>reviewers, now known as inspectors, to determine if a restaurant was a fine dining establishment. The ratings put in place in the</li>
  <li>1930s stand to this day—one star means the restaurant is “worth a stop,” two means it’s “worth taking a side trip,” and three means</li>
  <li>it’s “worth a special journey.”</li>
  <li class="is-start">Any restaurant under consideration will have served multiple inspectors, who decide whether the establishment delivers a</li>
  <li>consistent experience in terms of product quality, mastery of cooking techniques and the personality of the chef. What’s more, a</li>
  <li>restaurant can lose the stars if it doesn’t maintain Michelin Star-worthy standards.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Surprisingly, there’s no evidence that the Michelin Guide ever increased tire sales. However, the brothers made Michelin</li>
  <li>prestigious not only for tires but also for travel and gourmet cuisine.</li>
</ol>
