<p class="en-heading">MONSIEUR TAN-TAN</p>

<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">In 1840, Monsieur Leborgne arrived at the Bicêtre Hospital in Paris. He was a twenty-one-year-old man who exhibited an</li>
  <li>unusual linguistic problem: Whenever he was asked a question, he would always answer by saying one syllable twice, <em>tan tan</em>, in</li>
  <li>conjunction with quite varied intonation and expressive gestures. For this reason, the patient was nicknamed Tan-Tan. He spent</li>
  <li>the rest of his life hospitalized. Through the years, his condition deteriorated until, eventually, the limbs on his right side were</li>
  <li>paralyzed. On April 12, 1861, he was transferred to the surgery ward to be treated for gangrene. It was then that Pierre Paul Broca,</li>
  <li>a doctor who worked at the hospital, met him.</li>
  <li class="is-start">One week earlier, Broca had participated in a meeting of the Anthropological Society. During this meeting, another doctor,</li>
  <li>Ernest Auburtin, presented some interesting studies about the possibility of identifying the location of language in the human brain.</li>
  <li>Auburtin was attempting to support the hypothesis that the brain did not work as a homogenous mass, at least when it came to</li>
  <li>higher functions such as language. However, many scientists opposed the idea fiercely.</li>
  <li class="is-start">It did not take long for Broca to realize that the case he had come across constituted strong evidence to resolve the scientific</li>
  <li>controversy. Although it was hard to determine exactly how much Tan-Tan understood, he clearly was able to interpret most of</li>
  <li>what he heard. He could count and understand time. And neither his tongue nor his facial muscles had been affected by the</li>
  <li>paralysis. In other words, Tan-Tan did not lack the cognitive or motor skills necessary for talking. His problem must have been,</li>
  <li>therefore, a language-specific disability.</li>
  <li class="is-start">Tan-Tan died on April 17, 1861, and Broca did an autopsy of his brain. After careful examination, he concluded that a lesion</li>
  <li>in Tan-Tan’s left frontal lobe must have been the cause of his loss of language. Broca had just discovered the first anatomic</li>
  <li>evidence for the localization of a specific brain function. Shortly thereafter, he gave a talk at the Anthropological Society that would</li>
  <li>change our conception of how the brain works. Although not all of Broca’s colleagues accepted his conclusions, the road to studying</li>
  <li>the biological basis of language had already been taken. Modern neuroimaging techniques have shown that what is now known</li>
  <li>as Broca’s area is critically involved in grammar processing, sentence construction, and the coordination of language-related</li>
  <li>functions.</li>
</ol>
