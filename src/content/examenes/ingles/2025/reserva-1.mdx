<ol class="en-text">
  <li class="is-start">“What are you going as this Halloween?” The discussion at home starts in the spring, takes all the summer, and usually settles</li>
  <li>towards the end of September. This year we have been through John Lennon, a clown, and Pikachu. We have finally settled on</li>
  <li>Willy Wonka for my son and Snow White for me. As for my daughter, you can bet she will be wearing the same costume she wore</li>
  <li>last year. In a moment of weakness at an American shopping centre, I had bought her a &#36;40 inflatable costume of a “dark spirit</li>
  <li>riding a dinosaur,” and she loved it. She was really excited at the school’s Halloween fair surrounded by kids disguised as sumo</li>

  <li class="is-start">This year is the first time in almost two decades that we will be in the UK for Halloween, and most people I talk to seem to</li>
  <li>suggest that Halloween preparations here are out of control. Current research proves that Halloween spending has tripled since</li>
  <li>2013, with an average spend of £25 a person, rising to £43 in London. And while these increases are predominantly pushed by</li>
  <li>gen Z and millennials, the data suggests that their elders are being brought along to the party behind them.</li>
  <li class="is-start">However, Americans still take Halloween much more seriously than the British. Every year entire streets in our Manhattan</li>
  <li>neighbourhood are transformed, culminating in the Hollywood-level effects and decorations put into the houses on 69th Street,</li>
  <li>which annually draws crowds from across the city. As far as I can tell, Britain has not gone this far yet. Most houses aren’t</li>
  <li>decorated, and it’s common to see online warnings against knocking on houses without clear signs that the occupants are into</li>
  <li>Halloween. Also, the costumes in Britain are still based on the conventionally spooky rather than on funny or outrageous images</li>
  <li>that pop into your head and that you spend weeks trying to make wearable. Shops are another story: In the supermarkets pumpkin</li>
  <li>spice and similar products can be found everywhere.</li>
  <li class="is-start">My American children have no doubt about it: Halloween in Britain cannot compare yet. When I asked if they thought British</li>
  <li>Halloween had caught up with its American equivalent, my daughter gave me the same pitying look she used the day she asked</li>
  <li>me: “You really don’t know what a meme is?”</li>
